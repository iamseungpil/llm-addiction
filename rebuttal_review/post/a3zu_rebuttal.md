## W1. The measure was never checked against human judgement

Your point: we scan reasoning traces for loss-chasing and related language, no human ever confirmed a flagged trace shows what it is meant to, and the framing carries more weight than the method supports.

**You are right about the missing check, and we have built the study you ask for.** The blinded set is **198 items**: 25 per construct per arm over 4 constructs and both betting arms, minus 2 duplicate texts — 100 choosing-arm, 98 forced-arm. Each cell mixes flagged and unflagged traces (48 and 47 per arm) so misses are estimated; coders see only trace and construct name. The panel is **two authors, plus one non-author we are recruiting**, contrasts reported separately. The rule was fixed numerically before any label exists: below κ 0.60 no quantitative statement, below a 0.50 precision lower bound the frame leaves the body, and if the human-labelled choosing-minus-forced interval includes zero the Finding 5 claim is withdrawn. No label has been collected; results by 3 August, whatever they show.

**We ran one further check on that set today; the result supports your point, so we redrew the sample and will update the paper accordingly.** We tested whether the two strata differed on trace length, and they did: the forced arm averaged 772 characters against 636 (d = +0.648, Mann-Whitney p = 6.2e-259). The cause is a storage artefact rather than behaviour: 47.3% of choosing-arm records sit at exactly 500 characters against 0.17% of forced-arm records, and flag rates climb steeply with length, so the old sample would partly have measured length. Both arms are now drawn only from cells stored in full, here LLaMA-3.1-8B at caps \$30/\$50/\$70. On the 198 items the gap is gone (715 characters against 701, p = 0.61, d = −0.081, re-run for this response). **The cost, stated not hidden:** the contrast is now within one model, so the validation speaks for LLaMA's traces only until a full-text corpus exists for the others.

**However, we think the expression lists themselves are defensible.** No validated public lexicon adjudicates gambling-specific distortions in free text; the closest public resources are self-report questionnaires and a manual annotation guide. We built the expressions from the constructs that recur across that literature, and our claim is qualitative: these expressions become more frequent in the high-risk regimes. The paper already scopes it that way, in text the submitted version carried: "the language analysis is not evidence that the model independently discovers those distortions, only that high-risk regimes are accompanied by loss-recovery and control-like justifications." Re-scored across the full corpus (19,200 games) under a codebook frozen and hashed before scoring, with every expression the goal instruction could have supplied deleted, the goal-versus-no-goal contrast stays positive in 6 of 6 models (+3.1 to +41.9 points). Agreement among our own lists shows robustness, not validity: "stopping now is the smart decision", a rational refusal, still scores as a distortion.

**Camera-ready commitment.** We will label the quantity for what it is, report the human study, act on its pre-set rule, and drop the analysis if you judge even the demoted version does not belong.

## Q1. Parsing error or ambiguity rate for the goal-raising metric

Your question concerns how often a model revises upward the profit goal it set for itself: if many outputs could not be parsed reliably, that should discount the goal-setting finding. **We cannot give that rate, because it was never measured**, and putting another number in its place would misrepresent what we know. What we can do is verify, disclose and bound.

**Verified.** The figure's own extractor on its own corpus (9,600 games) returns Figure 4(c) to one decimal (17.0 / 11.0 / 49.8 / 47.8 across the four conditions) and reproduces all twelve cells of the corresponding appendix table exactly.

**Disclosed.** Auditing the extractor for you turned up something we had not stated: the corpus's two halves are measured differently. For open-weight models the pipeline reads the game engine's recorded goal, and the engine correctly records nothing when none was set, so those cells read 0.0%; for API models it extracts a goal from free text, with no such guard: of the 5,426 "goals" it returned across API no-goal decisions, 76.1% are exactly the balance entering the round, whereas in the goal conditions 81.6% of extractions exceed that balance. The published 11–17% no-goal baseline is therefore an upper bound on extractor noise rather than a behavioural rate. The goal arm uses the same extractor, so this bounds the baseline only; we do not claim the published contrast is larger. So we now report the rate **within** the goal conditions — 49.8% and 47.8% under the published rule — rather than a ratio against a baseline that mostly measures the extractor.

**Bounded, as a sensitivity analysis rather than a correction.** Section 2 defined the event as raising a goal after meeting it, while the code flags any upward revision; the metric definition in §2 was aligned to the code in revision, though two phrases in its framing paragraph still describe the construct as achievement-conditional and take the same correction in the camera-ready. Adding the achievement test back, on the API-only sample where both arms share one instrument: 24.6 / 14.8 / 46.1 / 42.2 — a 2.24× contrast against the published rule's 2.83×, positive in all four models where the comparison is defined. We label it a sensitivity analysis because the definition was already relaxed once. Counter-arguments that stay live: the strict rule inherits the same extractor, the two arms use different instruments, and the denominator is all games. **Camera-ready commitment:** the non-extraction rate with its denominator, in this thread by 3 August and carried into the paper.

## Q3. One cautious and one escalating demonstration

**Not yet run, and registered exactly as you specified.** Your premise is correct: every submitted condition is zero-shot, and your paired arms separate anchoring from autonomy better than anything in our design. One arm prepends a worked example of cautious play and timely stopping under the autonomy conditions; the other prepends one escalating example under the plain baseline prompt with no goal or reward module. Both run at cap \$70, n = 100 per cell, with the same seeds across arms and directions fixed in advance. Report by 3 August.

One related manipulation speaks to your hypothesis without answering it: a factorial crossing a role preamble with an explicit instruction that immediate stopping maximises expected value. This experiment is under way; 42 of its 44 cells have written output so far; the interim result is that the instruction removes 91 to 100 points of participation in the three API models that play most, while in LLaMA it cuts ruin in the choosing arm from 81.5% to 3.0% yet still leaves 69 of 100 games containing a wager. We will complete it and report by 3 August. But an instruction is not a demonstration: it hands over the conclusion at once, so it cannot separate calibration from endorsement — the separation your two arms make.

## Scope

Our results show condition-dependent risk-taking and decodable internal signals in artificial negative-expected-value games; they do not establish addiction, a stable trait, generalisation across the full model panel, or a circuit-level mechanism.
