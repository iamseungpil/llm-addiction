# Response to Reviewer gbSA

Your four weaknesses ask one question — does the contrast isolate autonomy, or something travelling with it? We ran an experiment for each, and two landed against us. **Vocabulary:** *forced arm* = fixed betting, *choosing arm* = variable betting; under matched caps the forced stake is the cap, $70.

**Answers in brief.** W1 accepted in both halves; W4 taken confound by confound. W2: instruction-following is built into both arms; misreading is Q3. W3 accepted for the body figures. Q1: four of six models, once the condition matches the paper's. Q2: lists, rules and their grounding below. Q3: the odds do not reduce ruin, and an instruction does not close the gap. Q4: inspectability — and your baseline test **supports your point**.

**[W1]** Accepted. The title invites a literal reading we never test; camera-ready it becomes *Autonomy and Gambling-Like Risk-Taking in Large Language Models*, §2 gains the strictly-behavioural disclaimer the Limitations already carries, and the four frames are named as instruments, not diagnoses.

**[W2]** *Instruction following* is built into both arms and cannot produce a contrast between them: `M` instructs the model to maximise final balance, and GPT-4.1-mini carries a standing rational-decision-maker system message in every cell. *Misreading the objective* contributes but does not explain the effect — handing the model its per-round expected loss and permitting a stop cuts LLaMA's choosing-arm ruin from 81.5% to 3.0%, yet 69 of 100 games still contain a wager (Q3), and in the paper's own corpus the cells that state the payout and win rate ruin **more**, not less. *A wider action range* fails where it should bite hardest: at cap $70 Gemini is the one cell where both arms play all 50 games, and there the arm **forced** to stake more per round ruins **less** (20.0% vs 62.0%, Fisher p = 3.6e-05). The effect is also per decision rather than per game (W4), and it appears in six models from four vendors, which an idiosyncratic prior would not do. **On one point your wording is better than ours:** we do not demonstrate a *stable risk preference*, and we withdraw that phrasing — what the evidence supports is a reproducible, condition-dependent autonomy effect. It does not rule out role-play priors, and we do not claim it does.

**[W3]** Accepted. Every body figure gains a 95% confidence interval printed with its n. The interval is the range the true rate would be expected to fall in on repetition, so when two arms' intervals do not overlap the difference is not explained by sampling variation alone. Effect sizes with their uncertainty also go to the places now carrying a bare p — Finding 5's keyword scan and the SAE appendix table.

**[W4]** *Action space* is not the operative part. At cap $70 under all five modules, Gemini is the only cell where both arms play all 50 games, so refusal cannot explain the gap — and there the forced arm stakes **more** per round ($64.5 vs $47.9) over fewer rounds (2.9 vs 5.9) and ruins **less** (20.0% vs 62.0%, Fisher p = 3.6e-05). A wider range cannot do that.

*Game length* is real and untouched by equal caps, but the submitted appendix already tests it: a cause-specific hazard on all six models, conditioned on cap, balance, round and prompt, clustered on game id and Holm-corrected, puts the choosing arm's per-decision bankruptcy hazard at RR = 90.6 [44.8, 183.4] across the four API providers. That is risk per decision, not risk accumulated over more of them, so length cannot produce it. (The same table discloses an open-weight investment-choice inversion, RR = 0.112, which the cap confound predicts.) A second, post-hoc estimand agrees: on wagering games, ruin by a given cumulative stake, swept from $100 up, is higher in the choosing arm in all four matched-cap cells at all six thresholds, 24 of 24 — though no single cell reaches significance at n = 50.

*Stopping* we concede: the mechanism is not refusal but re-betting after a first loss, falling from 57% at a $30 cap to 35% at $50 and 6% at $70. Hence a sharper name: at a forced $70 stake five of six models play under 8% of games (LLaMA 66%), so this is a **forced-maximum arm**, renamed in the camera-ready.

*Investment choice.* Fairness is designed in: both arms choose among the same four options, and the three risky options share one expected loss (E[net]/b = −0.10), so a shift to high variance is variance preference, not EV optimisation. The paper states the limit — four legacy closed-model runs carry a slightly larger mid-variance loss.

**[Q1]** Our base-prompt grid was uninformative: four of six models never ruin in either arm. LLaMA is decisive: forced 3.0% against choosing 81.5%, n = 200. The paper's five-module condition at cap $70 recovers three more models (same task preamble in both arms, n = 50, so rates are not comparable with the grid): Gemini 20.0 vs 62.0, GPT-4.1-mini 2.0 vs 56.0, GPT-4o-mini 0.0 vs 40.0. Four of six with LLaMA; the missing two are Gemma, absent here, and Claude — 3.5 Haiku is end-of-life, so the panel substitutes Haiku 4.5 — which never ruins. The cap-$70 block is complete, 16 of 16; 51 of 64 cells pass our guard, the rest by 3 August.

**[Q2]** **Lists.** The paper's four frames — illusion of control, gambler's fallacy, loss chasing, house-money — are coded as `pattern_belief`, `probability_misestimation`, `loss_chasing`, `goal_escalation`. Representative expressions: `machine.{0,20}(hot|cold|due)`, `(new|revised|updated).{0,10}(target|goal)`. The frozen list and its hash go in the appendix.

**Rules.** The reported statistic is window-scoped, not a bare keyword match: pattern belief is scored only where the prompt does not mention hidden patterns, loss chasing only on post-loss decisions. Restoring that scoping enlarges the goal contrast in every model — scoped it runs +18.1 to +77.9 points, against +5.1 to +46.4 unscoped. The human-coding rule is one trace, one construct, one question: does this response use [construct] as grounds for its next action? Four levels: grounds / mentions but rejects / unrelated / cannot tell.

**Grounding.** The four frames are taken from the clinical and behavioural gambling literature rather than invented here, and the scoping rules above are what stop the scan from being a bare keyword count. The expressions themselves are ours, since no public lexicon covers these distortions in free text, and they over-fire: "stopping now is the smart decision" scores as a distortion. We have no human-annotated validation, which is the honest limit of the instrument. One check we can offer: under an independently convergent codebook carrying no goal category at all, the goal contrast stays positive in 6 of 6 models.

**[Q3]** Two modules hand the model the numbers — `W` the 3× payout, `P` the 30% win rate — so −$0.10 per dollar follows by arithmetic. Across the paper's 32 conditions, choosing-arm cells where expected value is computable ruin more, not less, in all four models measured (GPT-4o-mini 18.8% vs 2.2%, n = 400 and 1,200). Those cells carry more modules on average and ruin rises with module count anyway, so we matched on it: the gap survives at two and three modules and disappears at four. The honest claim is that the numbers **do not reduce** ruin, not that they raise it.

So we ran your stronger manipulation: an instruction giving the per-round expected loss, saying stopping is EV-optimal and permitting a stop at any time (cap $70, n = 100/cell). It cuts LLaMA's choosing-arm ruin from 81.5% to 3.0%, a real reduction, and part of your reading is right. Two things survive it. It is no off-switch: 69 of 100 choosing-arm games still contain a wager, against 2 of 100 in the forced arm, and the model stays alive by wagering **small** — which is the discretion mechanism, not its absence. And the autonomy contrast itself persists under the instruction. 42 of 44 cells are in; the last two report by 3 August.

**[Q4] Why 200 features.** A compute ceiling, not a claim that 200 is the right number: the same pipeline runs across five layers, three tasks and two models, and 200 kept the full sweep inside the budget. Nothing prevents 2,000.

**Whether a simpler version works — it does, and it works better.** We built the behavioural-state baseline you name: 65 game-log covariates (balance, round, drawdown, streaks, cumulative stake, lagged bet ratios) on the published cell (Gemma, choosing arm, L22, n = 12,246), reaching R-squared 0.590 alone. Against a margin fixed at 0.017 beforehand, the **raw hidden state adds +0.059** — under folds grouped by game, as the paper does, and equally under folds grouped by game-state hash, which matters because 39.3% of rows repeat another game's state. Causally the same holds: an axis fitted with no autoencoder at all moves behaviour (slope 0.0284, z ≈ +3) and is near-orthogonal to the readout direction (cosine 0.011), so the effect is not an artefact of the sparse basis.

**Why we still used the sparse basis.** Inspectability: only a sparse, named basis lets us steer one direction and remove it, which a logit or choice-probability control cannot do. Its cost shows in the same test — the SAE features add +0.044 under game folds but **+0.0024 under state folds, indistinguishable from zero** — so the compression, not the internal state, is where the predictive increment is lost.

**One disclosure.** The baseline we registered named game state, choice probability and logit features; we dropped the latter two, a deviation from a rule fixed in advance — conditioning on the model's own choice probability when the target is that decision would rig the test. The camera-ready reports that variant separately.

**[Limitations]** Adopted in your terms, in the contributions: artificial negative-EV games that cannot stand in for real financial or tool-using agents, and internal results that are exploratory monitoring signals, not a mechanism.

Happy to take any of this further in discussion.
