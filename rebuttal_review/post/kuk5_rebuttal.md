# Response to Reviewer KuK5

Thank you for reading the paper closely. Both weaknesses pointed at real gaps, and we ran additional experiments for each rather than argue from what we had. Two of those runs support your position and we say so below; one is still finishing, and we give its interim state and a date.

**Summary first.**

- **W1 — can the neural decoding support a mechanistic reading?** No, and the submitted abstract already says so. But the null holds for one of two directions: the direction fitted to predict betting does not move behaviour, while a direction defined from the model's own betting moves it monotonically across a seven-point dose ladder.
- **W2 — does the equal-cap claim rest on one model?** It did. We have now run both arms capped at the same maximum bet across the panel, twice, and the dissociation appears in four of six models once the prompt condition matches the paper's.
- **Q1 — does it hold on the other five?** Partly: Gemini and GPT-4.1-mini are new models that show it under the paper's own condition, GPT-4o-mini reproduces it under those conditions in a fresh run, and LLaMA shows it in the base grid — four of six in total. Gemma is absent from this panel and the substitute Claude never ruins in either arm, so neither yields a testable contrast.
- **Q2 — what would a positive result have looked like, and could the fitted direction track a balance/round correlate?** Four criteria, stated below. Balance and round are already removed from the published R-squared inside each training fold, and the paper says so — but your question also implicates the betting history, above all the previous wager. That we had not tested; we have now, and **the result supports your point**.

## [Weakness 2 & Question 1] The equal-cap test across the panel

**You were right that one model cannot carry this claim**, and running the panel both extended the result and exposed a blind spot in our design.

**Our first extension was mostly uninformative**, and the reason matters: the paper's test aggregates all 32 prompt conditions at each cap, whereas this grid ran the base prompt only. Six models, four caps, both arms, n = 200 per cell, 48 of 48 complete — and four of six never go bankrupt in either arm there, one being Claude, whose pre-fix cells we do not rely on. LLaMA is decisive: at cap $70 the forced arm stakes $68.4 per round and ruins in 3.0% of games, while the choosing arm stakes $32.1 and ruins in 81.5%. The arm offered the larger stake survives, the opposite of what a range-expansion account predicts. Both pre-registered rules failed and we report them as failed. The primary was ill-posed, since the forced arm sits near 0% almost everywhere; our output wrongly recorded it as passing on an unregistered pooled interval, which we withdraw. The secondary (4 of 6 models wagering over half the cap and playing five times as many rounds) is 0 of 6: models given the choice wager only 22-46% of the cap.

**Restoring the paper's condition recovers the effect in two further models plus a fresh replication of GPT-4o-mini.** These are separate cells: the grid above is the base prompt at n = 200 with no persona; these run the four API models under all five prompt modules at cap $70, n = 50, **with the persona preamble present in both arms**, so Gemma is absent from this panel and LLaMA is carried over from the grid. Absolute rates are not comparable across the two, but the contrast within each is valid because the persona is constant across arms. Ruin, forced versus choosing: Gemini 20.0 vs 62.0% (+42 points), GPT-4.1-mini 2.0 vs 56.0 (+54), GPT-4o-mini 0.0 vs 40.0 (+40), and 0.0 vs 0.0 for the substitute Claude; Gemini shows it under the base prompt too (6.0 vs 34.0). With LLaMA, four of six.

**The cleanest cell.** Gemini with all five modules is the only cell where both arms play all 50 games, so refusal cannot explain the gap; there the forced arm stakes more per round ($64.5 vs $47.9) and ruins less (Fisher p = 3.6e-05). Your request also surfaced a limit of our own: equal caps equalise the per-round maximum, not the total staked. Compared at equal cumulative stake, the choosing arm still ruins more in all four cells at all six thresholds, 24 of 24, though intervals overlap within each cell at n = 50 and the sweep is post-hoc.

One disclosure: Claude 3.5 Haiku is end-of-life and returns 404, so this panel substitutes Claude Haiku 4.5, a different model; no pre-fix Claude figure is quoted above. 51 of 64 cells now pass our data-quality guard, including all 16 cap-$70 cells — those quoted; we will report the rest by 3 August. In the camera-ready that section becomes this panel result plus the equal-stake sweep, with the failed rules printed as failed.

## [Weakness 1 & Question 2] What the internal-state results do and do not show

**We agree with your reading of the fitted direction, and the paper claims nothing more.** The abstract states the analysis "does not claim circuit-level mechanism", so there is no mechanistic claim to retract, and we accept your effect-size characterisation. The nulls are ours and we published them. However, they are too strong as a statement about the activations, and the reason is our intervention method.

**Repairing that method changes the outcome.** The original edits acted at one layer; the patching arm additionally patched a single token position and replaced the whole residual stream with the autoencoder's lossy reconstruction of it, so the model saw an approximation of every direction rather than a clean edit of one. That one layer is not enough is our own new finding, not something the submitted paper reports. Editing the raw hidden state across a six-layer band instead gives a dose response on Gemma for the behaviourally defined axis (built from the model's own betting, not fitted to predict it) that is monotone across all seven doses, from 0.009 at the negative extreme through 0.182 unperturbed to 0.286 at the positive, at 200 games per dose; LLaMA moves the same way.

**The direction you are asking about stays null even after the repair**, at steering z = +0.75 in the same band and removal p = .885 on Gemma and p = 1.0 on LLaMA. The repair shows the activation space is not causally inert, while leaving your reading of the fitted direction intact. What passes both tests is the behavioural axis above: dose slope 0.0457 at z = +4.45 against a null of **twenty matched random directions**, and removal lowers betting by 0.037 on Gemma and 0.052 on LLaMA on **200 seed-matched pairs each**.

**What a positive result would have looked like (Q2).** Four criteria, fixed before the runs: (1) steering up raises the betting index and steering down lowers it, with a bootstrap interval on the difference excluding zero; (2) the effect grows across the ladder rather than appearing only at the extreme dose; (3) it clears a null band of matched random directions; (4) parse success does not degrade with dose. The fitted direction meets (2), meets (3) at one dose in one direction only, and (1) is untested; we do not count an untested criterion in our favour, so it is not a control lever.

**Your residualisation question, in two parts.** Balance and round are already removed in the published number. The appendix caption on the 0.167 cell states, in the submitted version, that balance and round count are statistically removed before fitting Ridge and that 5-fold GroupKFold by game id follows; the layer-sweep caption adds that this deconfound runs **within fold**.

But residualising two variables is not a nested comparison against a rich observable baseline, and it leaves the betting history in — above all the previous wager of an autocorrelated policy. **We ran this additional experiment, the result supports your point, and we will update the paper accordingly.** On the same 12,246 decisions across 1,596 games as the published cell, we built a 65-covariate game-log baseline (balance, round, drawdown, outcome streaks, cumulative stake, and lagged bet ratios including the previous wager). The reproduction gate passes at 0.16736 against the published 0.167. But 4,808 rows (39.3%) share a bit-identical game state with a row in a *different* game, so grouping by game id does not keep a state off both sides of a split. Grouping by state hash instead, on held-out rounds the raw hidden state recovers the policy better than the 65-covariate log (+0.0591; 99.5% of 200 resamples clear our pre-set 0.017 margin). We read that as the hidden state supporting a richer functional recovery of the same policy, not as proof that it holds information the log lacks — a 3,584-dimensional basis over the same state variables would do as much. **The sparse-autoencoder features the paper fits its direction from do not even reach that bar: +0.0024, indistinguishable from zero** (median -0.034, 29.5% clearing). In the metric the published 0.167 is actually in — R-squared on the deconfounded residual — the same features do add +0.037 to +0.045 over the game log, and that increment survives state grouping; the failure is specific to the raw-indicator target.

**Scope.** Our results show condition-dependent risk-taking and decodable internal signals in artificial negative-expected-value games; they do not establish addiction, a stable trait, generalisation across the full panel, or a circuit-level mechanism.

**Camera-ready.** Report the nested-baseline result including the cell that fails, disclose that we dropped choice probability and logits from the registered baseline (conditioning on the model's own choice probability when the target is that decision would rig the test), and print the four criteria and the repaired ladder.
