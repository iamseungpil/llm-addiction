# Response to the Area Chair

Thank you for the summary. We read the metareview as three concerns and answer them in that order: whether the clinical framing claims more than the paper shows, whether the behavioural evidence is fair and general enough to carry it, and whether the neural evidence is causal or only predictive. Everything below was run during the response period.

| experiment run for this response | what it decides | result |
|---|---|---|
| equal-cap grid, five models | does the fixed-variable dissociation hold beyond one model? | the variable arm ruins at least as often in 29 of 32 pairs; 9 of 12 API differences exclude zero, none negative |
| policy-choice experiment, four arms | is the risk induced by autonomy, or by something narrower? | a self-chosen stake behaves like a forced one, +3.0 pp; only per-round discretion raises ruin, +80.0 pp; widening the range does nothing, −5.0 pp |
| behavioural-axis intervention and removal, two models | can anything in the activation space be shown to change betting? | betting rises with dose and falls when the component is removed, in both models; the submitted readout stays inert |
| demonstration experiment, 16 API cells | does one worked example move behaviour? | in the one cell with room, a cautious example cuts ruin to 21% and an escalating one raises it to 52% |

The title claims a psychiatric state the paper never tested, so it goes: the camera-ready is *Gambling-Like Risk-Taking in Large Language Models*, with the diagnostic vocabulary replaced throughout. That is a subtraction of claims and needs no new data. The language analysis that carries the framing is the part a3Zu found unconvincing, and we agree it was never checked against human judgement. What we could test is whether the result depends on expressions written against our own corpus, and it does not. The revision is built on one frozen codebook whose four analysis categories follow definitions from the gambling-cognition literature the paper already cites; the expressions are our operationalisation of those definitions, fixed before any statistic and applied unchanged everywhere. It reproduces the contrast in all six models. Human annotation is registered for the camera-ready and we do not claim it now.

The behavioural concern was testable, and the answer narrows what we claim. Holding the maximum stake equal, a model that must wager the cap ruins less often than one free to choose any wager up to that same cap, so range expansion does not explain the gap. Letting the model set its own stake once, and separately widening the range, shows what does: being given a choice is not what carries the risk, revisiting the choice every round is. The matched-cap test now covers five models rather than one, at four caps for the API models, and both pre-registered panel rules were negative, so what we claim is a controlled dissociation rather than a panel-wide effect.

On the neural evidence we accept the reading. The fitted readout is a monitoring signal, not a demonstrated cause, and the submission says so itself. Rather than reinterpret that null we ran the intervention that would settle it: a separately defined direction, a dose ladder, a removal arm, random-direction controls and a validity gate. That direction moves behaviour; the submitted readout does not, under steering or removal. It does carry information beyond the visible game, which is what a monitoring signal is, and we keep the two apart. Every headline difference now carries a 95% interval on the difference itself rather than on two marginals.

No human control group played this game, no human coder has checked the flagged text, and we offer no mechanistic account of the readout. Where new data cut against our earlier wording we say so ourselves: the balance/round direction also steers on LLaMA, two of the four language categories do not move consistently, and part of what the fixed arm buys is stopping early rather than betting better.
