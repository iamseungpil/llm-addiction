# Response to the Area Chair

Thank you for the summary. The contribution of this paper is not a judgement about whether an LLM has a psychiatric condition. It implements, in an LLM environment, the conditions that human gambling research associates with risky decision-making, and studies how a model's participation, wager size, persistence and stopping change with the prompt and the structure of the choice. We read the metareview as raising three concerns: whether the current framing carries a stronger clinical claim than the evidence supports; whether the fixed-variable difference depends on wager size, stopping behaviour, choice structure or on a single model; and whether the neural readout is a cause of behaviour or a predictive signal. To answer them we set out what the submitted paper already shows and added a cross-model matched-cap experiment, a comparison between setting the wager once before play and choosing it again every round, a rationality instruction, an in-context demonstration experiment, a behavioural-state prediction baseline, and activation steering with component removal.

The first concern we answer by making the title and the vocabulary behavioural. The present title can be read as claiming that an LLM has a human-like mind and undergoes a genuine pathology. The camera-ready title becomes *Gambling-Like Risk-Taking in Large Language Models*, and diagnostic wording is replaced by behavioural description of participation, wagering, persistence and stopping. This retracts neither the research question nor the findings: what the submitted paper analysed was never a subjective experience but condition-dependent behaviour of the same model under different prompts and environments. Our contribution is to show that a model trained on human language can make irrational choices under conditions that human risk research treats as risky, and that this behaviour is amplified or suppressed by how the prompt and the environment are designed.

The second concern we answer with experiments that control the behavioural result more directly. The submitted matched-cap analysis compared one model on the slot machine at caps of \$10, \$30, \$50 and \$70, and found the variable condition staking less per round while playing longer and going bankrupt more often at every cap above \$10. That analysis could not tell us whether the same difference appears in other models, or how stopping and per-round revision each contribute. We therefore ran the same four-cap comparison on further API models, and in most comparisons the fixed condition staked more per round and still went bankrupt less often. We then added a condition in which the model sets its wager once before play and cannot revise it. It behaved like the fixed one, while letting the amount be chosen again each round raised bankruptcy and re-betting sharply, and widening the range added nothing further. The result therefore supports a narrower reading than autonomy in general: what tracks the risk is the structure that lets a decision be revised after a loss, together with stopping behaviour, rather than the existence of a choice or the size of the maximum stake.

The third concern we answer by separating the predictive role of the submitted readout from a separate intervention. The submitted SAE readout fits a Ridge model on sparse features of decision-time activations in two models and three tasks, and the paper already reports the effects as small to medium; that result alone cannot establish a cause. We first checked whether the hidden representation still adds held-out predictive information once observable game information such as balance, round, recent losses and previous wagers is controlled. We then built, separately from the submitted readout, a behavioural axis that distinguishes high-bet from low-bet decisions, and moved only that direction while holding prompt, seed and game state fixed. In both Gemma and LLaMA the bet ratio rose with the dose and fell when the component was removed. In Gemma neither random directions nor a balance-and-round control direction reproduced this two-sided pattern. In LLaMA the state-related direction also produced a steering effect, so the specificity of the direction is weaker there. We therefore keep the submitted readout as a monitoring signal and report the behavioural-axis experiment separately, as evidence that a direction able to change behaviour exists in the activations.

These additions do not replace the paper's original contribution with a different claim; they make its meaning and scope more accurate. We do not claim that an LLM has a human-like mind or a context-independent risk preference. We show that the risky behaviour of a given model varies systematically with the prompt and the structure of the choice, that this variation is not explained by the maximum stake or by a single act of choosing, and that behaviour-related internal information is observable in prediction and under intervention. That stopping behaviour is an important part of the condition difference, and that the specificity of the neural intervention differs by model, will be stated plainly in the revision. We are sorry that preparing and checking these experiments delayed our reply, and grateful for questions that let us state the contribution and its limits more precisely.
