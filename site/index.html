Can an AI get hooked on gambling?

Let a language model choose its own bet, and it goes broke far more often.

Scene 2

What makes it risky?

In a separate LLaMA test we added one freedom at a time, with bets capped at $70 and then at $100.

Bankruptcy jumps once the model can change its bet every round.

illustration

A goal it sets itself keeps moving.

≈19% → ≈36%In a second game with three losing gambles, models asked to set their own profit goal went bankrupt nearly twice as often (six models pooled).

Once they reached a goal, they often raised it instead of stopping.

A goal that should end the game becomes a reason to keep going.

Scene 3

A dial inside the model

Before the outcome, the hidden state already carries a weak but real signal of risk. Adding a direction built from the model's own bets changes how much it wagers, most clearly on Gemma. The direction that reads risk best leaves the bet where it was.

The dial is the model's average hidden state on its highest-betting rounds minus the average on its lowest, with the effect of balance taken out.

What this shows

  • Gambling-like behaviour, scored with clinical criteria, in small and mid-sized open and API models.
  • Comparisons within each model, plus an edit to one direction in the two open models that moves the wager.

What it does not claim

  • That models feel craving, or that their rates can be set against people's.
  • A circuit-level mechanism, or anything about frontier-scale models.
How we measured it

Six models (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude-3.5-Haiku, LLaMA-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-9B) played the slot machine under 64 conditions × 50 games: the two betting rules crossed with every on/off combination of five prompt modules. A second game, investment choice, tests self-set goals. On Gemma-2-9B and LLaMA-3.1-8B we read each decision's hidden state through sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features, and steer by adding a direction to that state.
